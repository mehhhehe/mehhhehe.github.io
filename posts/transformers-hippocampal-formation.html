Neuro7 min read

Relating Transformers to Models and Neural Representations of the Hippocampal Formation

authorsJames C. R. Whittington, Joseph Warren, Timothy E. J. Behrens
venueICLR 2022
paperarXiv:2112.04035 →

I put this one last because it closes a loop that runs through this whole notebook. We started with the Transformer, an architecture built by engineers, for machine translation, with no reference to biology. This paper shows that same architecture reproducing some of the best-characterized representations in neuroscience. The architecture we built to process language turns out to resemble what the brain uses to navigate space.

The brain side

The hippocampal formation is the brain's map-maker. Two of its most studied cell types: place cells, which fire when you're in a particular location, and grid cells, which fire on a regular triangular lattice across an entire space, the periodic coordinate system I wrote about in the grid-cells note. Neuroscience has its own models of how these arise, built by hand to capture this spatial machinery.

The bridge

Whittington and colleagues show that a Transformer, given the right ingredient (recurrent position encodings), develops internal representations that match place cells and grid cells. Not loosely, but the precise spatial codes neuroscientists recognise. And it isn't a coincidence. The transformer's mechanism is mathematically close to existing hippocampal models from neuroscience; it's effectively a more general version of the same computation. Two communities, working from opposite ends (one optimizing translation, one reverse-engineering memory), arrived at the same computation.

The architecture built to handle language ended up reproducing the brain's code for space.

The traffic runs both ways. The transformer version doesn't just match the neuroscience model; it outperforms it, which makes it a useful scientific instrument, a sharper hypothesis about what the hippocampal-cortical system might be doing. The authors suggest the same style of computation could extend beyond space into relational, structured tasks that wider cortex handles: memory, inference, possibly language comprehension itself.

Why this is where I stop

Everything in these notes circles one idea: that minds and models, pushed toward similar problems, keep arriving at similar solutions, from grid cells emerging in trained agents, to recognition networks predicting visual cortex, to the Transformer landing on the hippocampus's own representations. This paper states that convergence most directly, because it links the most important architecture in modern AI to the brain's spatial code. It's the intersection I want to work at: not "AI or neuroscience," but the place where each one becomes a tool for understanding the other.

What I took away

  • Place cells and grid cells are the hippocampal formation's code for location and space.
  • A Transformer with recurrent position encodings reproduces those precise spatial representations.
  • It's not luck: the mechanism is closely related to existing neuroscience models, and outperforms them.
  • The most important architecture in modern AI doubles as a hypothesis about how the brain maps the world.

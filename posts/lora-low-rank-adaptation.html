LoRA: Low-Rank Adaptation of Large Language Models

authorsEdward Hu, Yelong Shen, Phillip Wallis, Zeyuan Allen-Zhu, Yuanzhi Li, Shean Wang, Lu Wang, Weizhu Chen (Microsoft)
venueICLR 2022
paperarXiv:2106.09685 →

If GPT-3 showed how powerful giant models are, this paper answers the obvious follow-up for anyone without a data centre: how do you actually adapt one? It's a practical paper, not a new frontier, but a useful idea that makes the frontier accessible. The kind of thinking I apply when I build pipelines that have to run on real budgets.

The problem with fine-tuning

The default way to specialise a pre-trained model is full fine-tuning: keep training, update every weight. For a 175-billion-parameter model that's expensive. You need enough memory to hold and update all of it, and every task you adapt to produces a brand-new 175B-parameter copy to store and serve. Ten tasks, ten full copies. It doesn't scale, and for a student or a small team it's out of reach.

The insight: updates are low-rank

The authors start from a hypothesis: when you adapt a big model to a task, the change to the weights, call it ΔW, has low "intrinsic rank." The update is far simpler than the full weight matrix it's applied to. So why represent it as a full matrix at all? Instead, write ΔW as the product of two skinny matrices, B·A, where the shared inner dimension (the rank) is tiny. Freeze the original weights entirely; train only those two small matrices.

Don't relearn the model. Learn the small correction that points it at your task.

The numbers are the practical part. Against full fine-tuning of GPT-3, this cut the trainable parameters by up to ten thousand times and GPU memory by roughly three times, while matching and sometimes beating full fine-tuning on quality. And because the original weights are untouched, the learned update can be merged in at deployment, so there's no extra inference latency. Switching tasks means swapping a few-megabyte adapter instead of reloading a hundred-gigabyte model.

What it changed for me

Two things. First, it put adapting large models within reach of people like me. LoRA is why a student can specialise a serious model on a single GPU, and that accessibility is why I use it. Second, the deeper lesson: look for the low-dimensional structure hiding in a problem before you pay full price. The expensive object (a giant weight matrix) had a cheap update living inside it; the whole win came from noticing that and not over-parameterising the change. I now ask that question, "is the thing I'm learning secretly simpler than the thing I'm changing?", across my own work, well beyond language models.

What I took away

  • Full fine-tuning of huge models is costly to train, store, and serve: a copy per task.
  • The weight update from adaptation is low-rank, so represent it as two small matrices.
  • Freeze the base, train the adapter: up to 10,000x fewer trainable params, no added inference latency.
  • The general move, find the low-dimensional structure before paying full price, travels far.

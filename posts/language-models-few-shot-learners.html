Language Models Are Few-Shot Learners

authorsTom Brown, Benjamin Mann, Nick Ryder, Melanie Subbiah, Jared Kaplan, Prafulla Dhariwal, et al. (OpenAI)
venueNeurIPS 2020
paperarXiv:2005.14165 →

This is the GPT-3 paper. It studies whether a large language model can perform new tasks from examples in the prompt, without updating its weights.

The setup

The authors scaled an autoregressive transformer to 175 billion parameters, over a hundred times bigger than its predecessor, and trained it on the straightforward objective of predicting the next token across a large slice of the internet. Nothing exotic in the recipe. The whole bet was on scale: more parameters, more data, more compute, same idea.

Then they evaluated it differently. Instead of fine-tuning on each task, they gave it in-context examples: a few demonstrations of input to output in the prompt, followed by a new input. Translation, question answering, arithmetic, unscrambling words, all framed as "here are a few examples, now you continue." No weight updates. The model infers the pattern and applies it.

Few-shot learning stopped being a training procedure and became something the model does at inference time.

Why it mattered, and the honesty I respect

The headline is emergence: this in-context ability isn't really present in small models and gets stronger as scale grows. A capability nobody explicitly trained for showed up as a side effect of size. That's the observation that oriented the field around scaling, and it's also what makes me cautious, because "it emerges if you make it bigger" is hard to reason about or control.

What I appreciate is that the paper doesn't oversell. It documents where GPT-3 is weak: arithmetic with more digits, tasks needing real multi-step reasoning, places where it's fluent but wrong. Holding those two things together, a real result and a clear list of failures, is the kind of scientific honesty I want to follow.

What it changed for me

The practical shift is that some tasks can be specified in the prompt instead of through a separate fine-tuning dataset. The broader point is about scale: increasing model size and data can produce new behaviours that are not obvious in smaller models.

What I took away

  • A 175B-parameter next-token predictor learns tasks from a few in-prompt examples, weights frozen.
  • This in-context ability is weak in small models and emerges with scale.
  • The paper is candid about failures (multi-digit arithmetic, real reasoning), which makes it more trustworthy.
  • "How much data to fine-tune on?" is now sometimes "can I just describe the task?"
